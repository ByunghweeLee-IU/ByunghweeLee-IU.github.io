    This lecture note connects the implementation level, starting from a small corpus, to “meaning structure → query–key learning → negative sampling → noise distribution → SGNS → exponential gravity law”.

    When you first encounter Word2Vec, you see the results. “cat” and “dog” are close, and “car” and “truck” are close. This is surprising, but it leaves you with important questions.

    Who told the two words that they are “similar”?

    No one told them. The model only received sentences and the event of “appearing together in the surrounding context”. This note traces how these sparse events become a map of meaning. Finally, you will be able to read the same learning again in the following equation.

    $$ \text{observed interaction} \approx \text{source mass} \times \text{destination mass} \times \exp(\text{latent attraction}) $$

    This equation is the point where Word2Vec and the exponential gravity law meet.

    30-Second Key Takeaways#

    Skip-gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) assigns each word two types of vectors.

    • input embedding: used when the word serves as the center: query vector $q_w$
    • output embedding: used when answering with a candidate: key vector $k_c$

    If cat asks and pet is considered a candidate answer, their scores are:

    $$ s(\text{cat},\text{pet}) = q_{\text{cat}}^\top k_{\text{pet}} $$

    This score is calculated as:

    $$ (\text{cat},\text{pet})\text{ is a real pair} \quad\Rightarrow\quad q_{\text{cat}}^\top k_{\text{pet}}\uparrow $$

    $$ (\text{cat},\text{road})\text{ is a negative pair} \quad\Rightarrow\quad q_{\text{cat}}^\top k_{\text{road}}\downarrow $$

    So far, we have “attraction and repulsion”. However, the really important thing is that many pairs share the same vector. For example, cat and dog both need to have high scores on pet, food, and home, so it is more efficient for their queries to take similar directions. Semantic similarity is not given as the correct answer, but rather as a result of repeatedly solving the same prediction problem.

    In this document, “query-key” is a modern interpretation of the term. In the original Word2Vec literature and implementations, it is usually referred to as “input/output embedding”. Although the query/key in the Transformer is similar in shape, the vectors in Word2Vec are static parameters attached to the word ID, and are generated differently depending on the context.

    1. Skip-gram learns “encounters”, not sentences#

    Suppose the window size is 2 in “the cat likes pet food.” If the center word is cat, then the context within two positions is:

    the, likes, pet

    Therefore, the positive pair is:

    (cat, the)
(cat, likes)
(cat, pet)

    By moving the center by one position in the same sentence and repeating it across the entire corpus, we get a large multiset of (center, context) pairs. If a pair appears 10 times, it is not just one learning event, but 10 learning events. The number of repetitions is the signal.

    raw sentences
    ↓ sliding window
(center, context, count)
    ↓
sparse word-context relation matrix

    This completes the first causal relationship.

    Sentences do not directly provide meaning. The window defines the “relationship”, and the repeated relationship is the learning signal.

    When the window size is small, it captures more local grammatical relationships, while when it’s large, it captures more words related to the same topic. Therefore, the embedding is a product of the corpus, not just the corpus, but also the “observation device” of corpus + window.

    Practice: How the window affects relationships#

    Consider the sentence “the dog enjoys warm home”. If “enjoys” is the center and the window size is 1 and 2, what contexts would be created? How would the relationships learned differ in the two settings?

    Simple solution With a window size of 1, "dog" and "warm" are created. With a window size of 2, "the" and "home" are also added. The former defines a more localized combination, while the latter extends the relationship to the topic of the sentence. In other words, the window does not simply define a simple calculation range, but rather defines "what to consider as a related relationship".

    2. Why does Word2Vec have two vectors?#

    Let $V$ be the vocabulary size and $d$ be the embedding dimension.

    There are two embedding matrices.

    $$ Q \in \mathbb{R}^{V\times d} $$

    $$ K \in \mathbb{R}^{V\times d} $$

    Q[w] is the vector used when the word $w$ appears as the center.

    $$ q_w = Q[w] $$

    K[c] is the vector used when the word $c$ appears as the context.

    $$ k_c = K[c] $$

    Therefore, the score in Word2Vec is

    $$ s(w,c)=q_w^\top k_c $$

    .

    This is very similar to the attention mechanism in Transformer, where

    $$ q_i^\top k_j $$

    is calculated.

    The difference is that in Transformer, the query/key are dynamically generated based on the input, while in Word2Vec, there is a static query/key vector that is learned for each token in the vocabulary.

    “Given a center word $w$, how compatible is the context candidate $c$?”

    The center word acts as a query.

    $$ q_w $$

    The context candidate acts as a key.

    $$ k_c $$

    compatibility:

    $$ q_w^\top k_c $$

    In the corpus village,

    center = cat

    we ask this question to the candidate key,

    pet
home
road
traffic

    let’s say it is

    If the learning is successful,

    $$ q_{\text{cat}}^\top k_{\text{pet}} $$

    $$ q_{\text{cat}}^\top k_{\text{home}} $$

    and

    $$ q_{\text{cat}}^\top k_{\text{road}} $$

    are large,

    then Word2Vec is a learned retrieval system that searches for a context key that is suitable for the query “cat”.

    4. The initial vectors have no meaning#

    Initially, the embeddings are randomly initialized.

    q_cat = [ 0.01, -0.03,  0.02 ]
k_dog = [-0.02,  0.04,  0.01]

    For example:

    Initially, there is no semantic structure at all.

    As the training process repeats the observed co-occurrence, a certain direction emerges.

    cat → likes, pet, food, enjoys, warm, home
dog → likes, pet, food, enjoys, warm, home

    The key is that if the same relationships are repeated, “cat” and “dog” should have high dot products with similar context keys.

    Therefore, the two query vectors naturally align in a similar direction.

    In other words, similarity is not directly enforced, but rather

    it is indirectly created because it is necessary to predict similar contexts.

    Ⅱ. Learning Dynamics of SGNS#

    ↑ Table of Contents

    5. Full Softmax Skip-gram#

    The original Skip-gram model has the following probability model:

    The probability of the context $c$ given the center word $w$:

    $$ P(c\mid w) = \frac{\exp(q_w^\top k_c)} {\sum_{c'=1}^{V}\exp(q_w^\top k_{c'})} $$

    In other words, all vocabulary candidates and scores are calculated.

    The log likelihood is

    $$ \log P(c\mid w) = q_w^\top k_c - \log \sum_{c'}\exp(q_w^\top k_{c'}) $$

    .

    The problem is that if the vocabulary is 100,000, we need to calculate 100,000 scores for each positive pair.

    Therefore, it is expensive.

    6. The idea of Negative Sampling#

    Negative Sampling changes the problem.

    Existing Problem:

    Which context among all words is the correct answer?

    Negative Sampling:

    Is this (center, context) pair a genuine pair from real data, or a fabricated pair from noise?

    In other words, it converts multiclass classification into binary classification.

    positive example:

    (cat, pet) -> y = 1

    negative example:

    (cat, road) -> y = 0
(cat, traffic) -> y = 0
(cat, load) -> y = 0

    score:

    $$ s=q_w^\top k_c $$

    probability:

    $$ P(D=1\mid w,c)=\sigma(q_w^\top k_c) $$

    Here,

    $$ \sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} $$

    is.

    Here, “negative” is not the correct answer defined as “words that have no semantic relationship in the world.” It is simply a contrast set drawn from a predefined noise distribution. It is possible that a real positive is also drawn as a negative. In large corpora, probabilistic learning signals generally work, and positive tokens can be rejected depending on the implementation.

    7. SGNS objective#

    Given a positive pair $(w, c)$,

    $$ n_1,\dots,n_K $$

    we have:

    The objective to maximize is:

    $$ \log\sigma(q_w^\top k_c) + \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log\sigma(-q_w^\top k_{n_i}) $$

    In terms of loss, it is:

    $$ L = -\log\sigma(q_w^\top k_c) - \sum_i \log\sigma(-q_w^\top k_{n_i}) $$

    8. What exactly does the gradient from a positive pair do?#

    Score for positive pair:

    $$ s=q_w^\top k_c $$

    loss:

    $$ L_+=-\log\sigma(s) $$

    Taking the derivative, we get

    $$ \frac{\partial L_+}{\partial s} = \sigma(s)-1 $$

    and

    $$ \frac{\partial s}{\partial q_w}=k_c $$

    therefore

    $$ \frac{\partial L_+}{\partial q_w} = (\sigma(s)-1)k_c $$

    gradient descent results in

    $$ q_w \leftarrow q_w - \eta(\sigma(s)-1)k_c $$

    i.e.

    $$ q_w \leftarrow q_w + \eta(1-\sigma(s))k_c $$

    this.

    Similarly,

    $$ k_c \leftarrow k_c + \eta(1-\sigma(s))q_w $$

    this holds.

    Therefore, the two vectors in a positive pair move in opposite directions.

    9. What does the gradient do for negative pairs?#

    For a negative sample $n$

    $$ L_-=-\log\sigma(-q_w^\top k_n) $$

    we should.

    $$ s_n=q_w^\top k_n $$

    if

    $$ \frac{\partial L_-}{\partial s_n} = \sigma(s_n) $$

    therefore

    $$ \frac{\partial L_-}{\partial q_w} = \sigma(s_n)k_n $$

    gradient descent:

    $$ q_w \leftarrow q_w - \eta\sigma(s_n)k_n $$

    and

    $$ k_n \leftarrow k_n - \eta\sigma(s_n)q_w $$

    that is, a negative pair moves in a direction that reduces the dot product of the two vectors.

    10. Important Intuition: Gradients are not always the same size#

    in a positive pair,

    $$ 1-\sigma(s) $$

    is the gradient strength.

    if $s$ is already very large,

    $$ \sigma(s)\approx1 $$

    then there is almost no update.

    that is, if the model already

    cat → pet

    knows it well, it does not make large changes.

    Conversely, if it is positive but $s \ll 0$,

    $$ 1-\sigma(s)\approx1 $$

    then it is strongly pulled.

    The same is true for negative pairs.

    If the dot product is already very low in the negative case,

    $$ \sigma(s)\approx0 $$

    then there is almost no update.

    Ultimately, SGNS modifies based on the pair that is being poorly evaluated.

    11. A Typical Training Step#

    For example,

    center = cat
positive context = pet

negative contexts:
road
traffic
load

    let’s say.

    forward:

    q = Q["cat"]

k_pos = K["pet"]

k_neg = [
    K["road"],
    K["traffic"],
    K["load"]
]

    positive score:

    s_pos = dot(q, k_pos)

    negative scores:

    s_neg = k_neg @ q

    loss:

    loss_pos = -log(sigmoid(s_pos))
loss_neg = -sum(log(sigmoid(-s_neg)))

loss = loss_pos + loss_neg

    after backpropagation,

    q_cat      → toward k_pet
k_pet      → toward q_cat

q_cat      → away from k_road
q_cat      → away from k_traffic
q_cat      → away from k_load

    moves.

    Let’s look at a single negative example for illustration. Let’s assume the learning rate is $\eta = 0.1$.

    $$ q_{cat}=[0.4,-0.2],\qquad k_{pet}=[0.1,0.5],\qquad k_{road}=[-0.3,0.2] $$

    Before updating, the scores and magnitudes are as follows:

    pair label score $s$ sigmoid change in $q_{cat}$
    (cat, pet) 1 -0.06 0.485 +0.1(1-0.485)k_{pet}
    (cat, road) 0 -0.16 0.460 -0.1(0.460)k_{road}

    When combining the two gradients for the same forward state, we obtain approximately

    $$ q_{cat}^{new} \approx [0.4,-0.2] +0.1(0.515)[0.1,0.5] -0.1(0.460)[-0.3,0.2] =[0.419,-0.183] $$

    something like this. Focus on the direction, not the absolute values of the numbers. Add the key directional component of pet and subtract the key directional component of road. Simultaneously, $k_{pet}$ and $k_{road}$ are also adjusted to match their opposite roles. Repeat this process millions of times, but each time, the same parameters are exposed to the requirements of multiple relationships.

    A positive pair does not necessarily need a positive score. Showing $K$ negative pairs shifts the optimal baseline by $K$. What matters is whether a relevant pair scores higher than an unrelated pair and whether the score reflects the data-to-noise ratio. The $-\log K$ term makes this precise.

    A quick exercise: Which pair will move the most?#

    The model incorrectly evaluates the positive pair as $s_+=-3$ and the negative pair as $s_-=3$. What are the respective scalar strengths of the $1-\sigma(s_+)$ and $\sigma(s_-)$? What happens if the signs of the scores are reversed?

    A simple solution $\sigma(3)\approx0.953$, and $\sigma(-3)\approx0.047$. Therefore, both the incorrectly evaluated positive and negative pairs receive a strong force of approximately 0.953. Conversely, if positive is 3 and negative is -3, both forces are small, approximately 0.047. SGNS tends to correct the "wrong" pair more significantly than the already well-trained, easy pairs.

    12. How do vectors create meaning?#

    This is the most important part.

    For example, suppose we have two words.

    cat
dog

    They share the following contexts in the corpus:

    pet
food
likes
home
warm
enjoys

    Therefore, during learning,

    $$ q_{cat}^\top k_{pet}\uparrow $$

    $$ q_{cat}^\top k_{home}\uparrow $$

    and

    $$ q_{dog}^\top k_{pet}\uparrow $$

    $$ q_{dog}^\top k_{home}\uparrow $$

    we get

    Therefore, the query vectors $q_{cat}$ and $q_{dog}$ should have similar projections for multiple keys.

    In other words,

    $$ QK^\top $$

    the two rows of the matrix become similar.

    This means that the two rows of $QK^\top$ become similar. This is a natural compression result that occurs when $K$ can represent the relationship structure and the low-dimensional optimization is well-suited, but it is not an equation that is guaranteed in all cases. If you use this intuition, you get

    $$ q_{cat}\approx q_{dog} $$

    something like this.

    Conversely, cat and car have different sets of keys that require high scores. cat is biased towards pet/home, while car is biased towards road/load. This shared context creates a common pressure that builds two neighborhoods in the corpus village.

    Ⅲ. From PMI to Gravity#

    ↑ Table of Contents

    13. Word2Vec factors a huge relationship matrix#

    Let’s consider the entire word-context relationship as a matrix.

    $$ M_{wc} $$

    Let $w$ be a word and $c$ be a context. The relationship strength is represented by

    Word2Vec does not explicitly create this, but

    $$ M_{wc}\approx q_w^\top k_c $$

    learns a low-rank representation that makes it

    matrix notation:

    $$ M \approx QK^\top $$

    where

    $$ Q\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d} $$

    $$ K\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d} $$

    and usually

    $$ d \ll V $$

    is.

    In other words, Word2Vec is essentially factorizing a very large word-context interaction matrix into a low-dimensional representation.

    14. Connecting SGNS and PMI: First, a special case#

    Levy & Goldberg’s important interpretation is that SGNS implicitly performs shifted PMI matrix factorization. However, the following clean formula only holds when the noise distribution is the same as the actual context unigram:

    $$ P_n(c)=P(c) $$

    In practice, when the noise distribution uses an exponent of $3/4$, a correction term is added as shown.

    PMI:

    $$ PMI(w,c) = \log \frac{P(w,c)} {P(w)P(c)} $$

    In an idealized SGNS where each $(w,c)$ score can be freely set, we get

    $$ s_{wc}^* = PMI(w,c)-\log K $$

    This is the pairwise optimum. In reality, $s_{wc}=q_w^\top k_c$ is constrained to be low-dimensional, so we cannot set every pair to its optimum independently. Instead, $Q$ and $K$ jointly approximate the entire matrix so that $q_w^\top k_c\approx s_{wc}^*$ as closely as possible.

    Here, $K$ is the number of negative samples. In other words, the dot product is not simply a similarity, but

    “How much more frequently do these two words appear together in reality, compared to their independent appearance?”

    This can be represented in log scale.

    15. Using PMI to count#

    In the corpus,

    $$ N(w,c) $$

    we have word-context pair counts.

    $$ N(w)=\sum_c N(w,c) $$

    $$ N(c)=\sum_w N(w,c) $$

    Let $N$ be the total number of pairs. Then

    $$ P(w,c)=\frac{N(w,c)}{N} $$

    $$ P(w)=\frac{N(w)}{N} $$

    $$ P(c)=\frac{N(c)}{N} $$

    Therefore,

    $$ PMI(w,c) = \log \frac{N(w,c)N} {N(w)N(c)} $$

    is.

    Therefore, the dot product learned by SGNS is approximately

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx \log \frac{N(w,c)N} {N(w)N(c)} - \log K $$

    is.

    16. Noise distribution: “Compared to what?”#

    The key point is that the noise distribution $P_n(c)$ defines the baseline against which the model evaluates the data.

    • Uniform noise: Assume that all words will appear equally often.
    • Unigram noise: Assume that words that appear frequently in the corpus will also appear frequently in the noise.
    • 3/4-smoothed unigram: Show frequent words frequently, but do not overwhelm them with the raw frequency.

    The choice widely used in Word2Vec is

    $$ P_n(c) = \frac{f(c)^{3/4}} {\sum_u f(u)^{3/4}} $$

    To see how the $3/4$ power softens the head of the distribution, consider these hypothetical frequencies.

    context raw frequency $f(c)$ unigram relative weight $f(c)^{3/4}$ relative weight uniform relative weight
    the 10,000 10,000 1,000.0 1
    pet 100 100 31.6 1
    comet 1 1 1.0 1

    The ratio of the:pet is 100:1 in raw unigram, but after 3/4 smoothing, it is approximately 31.6:1. This reduces the dominance of frequent words like the while also preventing rare words from being over-represented.

    The 3/4 value is not just a mathematical constraint, but also a practical choice that has worked well in terms of balancing calculation efficiency and quality. The noise that is more appropriate may depend on the corpus and the purpose.

    Now, let’s ask the key question in the corpus village.

    • The word “the” appears frequently around “cat.” However, since it is common, this is not surprising.
    • The word “pet” appears less frequently overall than “the,” but it appears more frequently when “cat” is present.
    • The word “road” is a normal word in itself, but it appears less frequently in the context of “cat.”

    Therefore, we need to compare the observed frequency with the baseline frequency, rather than the raw count.

    $$ \frac{P_{\text{data}}(c\mid w)}{P_n(c)} $$

    This is the transition from negative sampling to the gravity law.

    17. The inner product of SGNS is the data-vs-noise log-odds#

    Positive pairs come from the actual pair distribution.

    $$ (w,c)\sim P_{\text{data}}(w,c) $$

    If we create $K$ negative pairs for each positive pair, then the noise pairs come in the following way:

    $$ w\sim P_{\text{data}}(w), \qquad c\sim P_n(c) $$

    Therefore, the relative frequency of the same $(w,c)$ in both worlds is:

    World Relative frequency of pair $(w,c)$
    Actual data $D=1$ $P_{\text{data}}(w,c)$
    Noise $D=0$ $K P_{\text{data}}(w)P_n(c)$

    The log-odds of the optimal Bayes classifier is:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \log\frac{P(D=1\mid w,c)}{P(D=0\mid w,c)} &= \log\frac{P_{\text{data}}(w,c)} {K P_{\text{data}}(w)P_n(c)}\\ &= \log\frac{P_{\text{data}}(c\mid w)} {K P_n(c)}. \end{aligned} $$

    SGNS attempts to represent this log-odds as a single inner product.

    $$ \boxed{ q_w^\top k_c \approx \log\frac{P_{\text{data}}(c\mid w)}{K P_n(c)} } $$

    It is important to read this equation aloud.

    The larger $q_{cat}^\top k_{pet}$ is, the more often “pet” appears near “cat” in the observed data than the noise baseline would predict. If “road” is frequently drawn from the noise distribution but rarely observed near “cat,” then $q_{cat}^\top k_{road}$ will be smaller.

    Strictly speaking, negative sampling is not exactly the same as the standard NCE, which aims to directly recover the normalized language model probability. However, the “noise-contrastive view” of “learning the log density ratio by classifying data and noise” is accurate and useful for understanding the above equation.

    Remember these two points.

    1. If each $s_{wc}$ can be treated as an independent parameter, then the optimal equation can be matched for each pair.
    2. In reality, there is a rank-$d$ constraint $s_{wc} = q_w^\top k_c$, so all pairs share a limited vector and approximate the entire log-odds matrix.

    It is precisely this constraint that leads to structural compression, or embedding, rather than simple memorization.

    18. Taking the logarithm results in the exponential gravity law#

    Now, we don’t need to artificially introduce a new model. We can simply exponentiate the equation we just obtained.

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx \log\frac{P_{\text{data}}(w,c)} {K P_{\text{data}}(w)P_n(c)} $$

    Taking the exponential of both sides and simplifying, we get:

    $$ \boxed{ P_{\text{data}}(w,c) \approx K\, P_{\text{data}}(w) P_n(c) \exp(q_w^\top k_c) } $$

    We can absorb the global constant and the normalization difference into $C$, which allows us to use the same notation for count or flow.

    $$ F_{wc} \approx C\,m_w m_c\exp(q_w^\top k_c), \qquad m_w=P_{\text{data}}(w),\quad m_c=P_n(c) $$

    These three components represent distinct roles.

    Term Role Meaning in the Corpus Village
    $m_w$ Mass/activity of the center How often does “cat” appear in the center?
    $m_c$ Context mass defined by noise How often does “pet” appear in the comparison target?
    $\exp(q_w^\top k_c)$ Pair-specific attraction How much better do “cat” and “pet” match, after removing their individual frequencies?

    In other words, the embedding does not simply memorize the raw co-occurrence, but compresses the residual attraction that cannot be explained by the basic activity levels of the two endpoints into a low-dimensional space.

    Unigram noise creates the most familiar gravity model#

    If $P_n(c) = P_{\text{data}}(c)$, then

    $$ P_{\text{data}}(w,c) \propto P(w)P(c)\exp(q_w^\top k_c) $$

    we have

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx PMI(w,c)-\log K $$

    where the inner product represents the unigram probability of each word, which plays the role of two masses.

    With $3/4$ noise, the destination mass changes#

    If the practical noise distribution is $P_n(c) = P(c)^{3/4}/Z_{3/4}$, then

    $$ P_{\text{data}}(w,c) \propto P(w)P(c)^{3/4}\exp(q_w^\top k_c) $$

    The same content can be represented by a score.

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx PMI(w,c)-\log K +\frac14\log P(c)+\log Z_{3/4}. $$

    19. Why is it called “gravity”?#

    The classic gravity model for spatial interaction is

    $$ F_{ij} = G\frac{m_i m_j}{r_{ij}^{\gamma}} $$

    like this. The more cities there are, the more the movement increases, and the farther apart they are, the less the movement decreases. Taking the logarithm gives

    $$ \log F_{ij} = \log G+\log m_i+\log m_j-\gamma\log r_{ij} $$

    This is a structure similar to the one obtained in Word2Vec.

    $$ \log F_{wc} \approx \log C+\log m_w+\log m_c+q_w^\top k_c $$

    destination $j$ context word $c$

    $$ F_{ij}\propto m_i m_j e^{-\beta d_{ij}} $$

    compared to SGNS, the affinity $q_i^\top k_j$ learned in place of $-\beta d_{ij}$ is used.

    $$ -\beta d_{ij} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad q_i^\top k_j $$

    However, the dot product itself should not be called a physical distance. Word2Vec does not receive distance as input. The data learns which latent attraction best explains the flow. Therefore, a more accurate representation is as follows:

    25. A Small NumPy Implementation#

    The following is a single step of SGNS, with only the core elements remaining.

    import numpy as np

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))


def sgns_step(
    Q,
    K,
    center_id,
    positive_id,
    negative_ids,
    lr=0.01
):
    # lookup
    q = Q[center_id].copy()
    k_pos = K[positive_id].copy()
    k_negs = K[negative_ids].copy()

    # -------------------------
    # forward
    # -------------------------

    s_pos = q @ k_pos

    s_neg = k_negs @ q

    loss_pos = -np.log(sigmoid(s_pos) + 1e-12)

    loss_neg = -np.sum(
        np.log(sigmoid(-s_neg) + 1e-12)
    )

    loss = loss_pos + loss_neg

    # -------------------------
    # backward
    # -------------------------

    # positive
    g_pos = sigmoid(s_pos) - 1

    grad_q = g_pos * k_pos
    grad_k_pos = g_pos * q

    # negatives
    g_neg = sigmoid(s_neg)

    grad_q += (g_neg[:, None] * k_negs).sum(axis=0)

    grad_k_negs = g_neg[:, None] * q

    # -------------------------
    # update
    # -------------------------

    Q[center_id] -= lr * grad_q

    K[positive_id] -= lr * grad_k_pos

    for idx, grad in zip(negative_ids, grad_k_negs):
        K[idx] -= lr * grad

    return loss

    This code represents almost everything in SGNS.

    2. To prevent the stopword, which appears with almost every word, from dominating the embedding geometry

    31. A More Accurate View of Negative Sampling and Matrix Factorization#

    Let’s say the number of positive pairs is

    $$ x_{wc} $$

    denoted as $N_{pos}$.

    The total number of times a word $w$ appears as a center:

    $$ x_w=\sum_c x_{wc} $$

    If the number of negative samples is $K$ and the noise distribution is $P_n(c)$, then the expected number of times the pair $(w, c)$ appears as a negative sample is

    $$ K x_w P_n(c) $$

    given by

    In the SGNS objective, the part corresponding to a specific pair $(w, c)$ is

    $$ x_{wc}\log\sigma(s_{wc}) + Kx_wP_n(c)\log\sigma(-s_{wc}) $$

    given by

    Here,

    $$ s_{wc}=q_w^\top k_c $$

    is

    We optimize this with respect to $s_{wc}$.

    Differentiating, we get

    $$ x_{wc}\sigma(-s) - Kx_wP_n(c)\sigma(s) = 0 $$

    Rearranging, we have

    $$ e^s = \frac{x_{wc}} {Kx_wP_n(c)} $$

    Therefore, the optimal value when the score can be freely set for each pair is

    $$ s = \log x_{wc} - \log x_w - \log P_n(c) - \log K $$

    given by

    $$ \boxed{ s_{wc}^* = \log \frac{P_{\text{data}}(c\mid w)} {K P_n(c)} } $$

    In a low-dimensional model, $q_w^\top k_c \approx s_{wc}^*$. The non-normalized optimal value for pairs where $x_{wc} = 0$ is $-\infty$, which limits the learning.

    This is one of the most powerful equations for understanding SGNS.

    32. Noise as Unigram Results in PMI#

    If

    $$ P_n(c)=P(c) $$

    then

    $$ q_w^\top k_c = \log \frac{P(c\mid w)} {P(c)} - \log K $$

    However,

    $$ \frac{P(c\mid w)}{P(c)} = \frac{P(w,c)} {P(w)P(c)} $$

    so

    $$ q_w^\top k_c = PMI(w,c)-\log K $$

    is obtained.

    In other words, the shifted PMI result is directly obtained.

    33. The Meaning of the Number of Negative Samples K#

    In the case of unigram noise and shifted-PMI,

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx PMI(w,c)-\log K $$

    so as $K$ increases, the overall score baseline decreases.

    For example:

    $$ K=5 \Rightarrow \log K\approx1.61 $$

    $$ K=20 \Rightarrow \log K\approx3.0 $$

    In other words, as the number of negative samples increases, stronger evidence is needed for a positive pair to receive a high score.

    Suppose the graph flow is

    $$ F_{ij} $$

    .

    In other words, the dot product of the embedding acts as a distance kernel or affinity term in the gravity equation.

    In a traditional gravity model, interaction is explained by observed geographic distance.

    $$ -\gamma\log r_{ij} $$

    In an embedding gravity model, this is explained by latent geometry.

    $$ q_i^\top k_j $$

    In other words:

    A traditional gravity model assumes that the interaction space is known in advance. An embedding model learns the interaction space itself from the data.

    38. Replacing Euclidean Latent-space Gravity#

    Instead of the dot-product model,

    $$ F_{ij} \propto m_i m_j e^{-\beta d_{ij}} $$

    $$ -\|z_i-z_j\|^2 = -\|z_i\|^2 -\|z_j\|^2 + 2z_i^\top z_j $$

    If we allow node-specific biases separately, the interaction part will eventually be transformed into a dot product form.

    In other words, the latent-distance model and dot-product embedding are deeply connected.

    $$ s_{ij} = b_i+b_j+q_i^\top k_j $$

    $$ F_{ij} \propto \exp(s_{ij}) $$

    $$ F_{ij} \propto e^{b_i} e^{b_j} e^{q_i^\top k_j} $$

    Here,

    $$ m_i=e^{b_i} $$

    $$ F_{ij} \propto m_i m_j e^{q_i^\top k_j} $$

    40. The True Role of Negative Sampling#

    If you only see negative sampling as a “trick to reduce computation,” you’re only getting half the picture.

    Negative sampling determines:

    “Is the observed relationship between two words surprising compared to a baseline?”

    For example, “the” appears frequently with many words.

    If you only look at the co-occurrence count,

    cat-the
dog-the
car-the

    it might seem like “the” has a strong relationship with all words.

    However, “the” also appears very frequently in the noise distribution.

    Therefore, “cat-the” is not very surprising.

    Conversely, “cat-pet” can appear much more frequently than in the baseline.

    This increases the dot product.

    In other words, SGNS learns not raw frequency, but

    $$ \text{observed interaction} / \text{expected baseline interaction} $$

    This is the key that connects PMI and the gravity model.

    48. The Meaning Lies in ‘Direction’ and ‘Magnitude’, Not a Single Coordinate#

    When you first see the embedding plot, you might want to interpret it like this:

    dimension 17 = animalness
dimension 42 = politics

    Generally, this is not how it should be interpreted. Because the coordinate system can rotate. For the orthogonal matrix $R$,

    $$ Q'=QR,\qquad K'=KR $$

    if you rotate them simultaneously,

    $$ Q'K'^\top = QRR^\top K^\top = QK^\top $$

    so all query–key scores remain the same. Even more strongly, for any invertible matrix $A$,

    $$ Q'=QA,\qquad K'=KA^{-\top} $$

    $Q'K'^\top=QK^\top$ also holds. In other words, the SGNS objective directly identifies the relationship score created by the query and key together, not individual coordinates.

    This does not mean that the geometric interpretation is meaningless. If you fix one learned space and one representation selection, the following three are very useful empirical structures.

    • Angle between two vectors: Similar relation profile?
    • Difference vector between two points: What changes from one concept to another?
    • Direction shared by multiple pairs: Are there recurring semantic relationships?

    Here, the semantic direction or meaning axis appears. The axis is not necessarily about finding the original “dimension 17”. Instead, it’s a newly defined direction within the current space, using the contrast between multiple words. If you rotate the coordinate system, both the words and the axis will rotate together, so the projection values remain the same.

    In practice, you first need to fix which word representation to use.

    $$ v_w=Q[w] $$

    or

    $$ v_w=\frac{Q[w]+K[w]}{2} $$

    You can combine these two. While neither is always correct, you should consistently use the same choice within a given analysis. If you carelessly mix different spaces by projecting the axis created from $Q$ onto the words of $K$, the interpretation will be incorrect.

    49. When the relationship becomes a direction: analogy, vector arithmetic, meaning axis#

    49.1 What does vector subtraction leave behind?#

    The difference vector between two words

    $$ d_{a\rightarrow b}=v_b-v_a $$

    contains how the relation profile changes when moving from $a$ to $b$. This intuition is clearer when using the log-odds interpretation of SGNS. When projecting onto a context key $k_c$,

    $$ \begin{aligned} (q_b-q_a)^\top k_c &=q_b^\top k_c-q_a^\top k_c\\ &\approx \log\frac{P(c\mid b)}{K P_n(c)} - \log\frac{P(c\mid a)}{K P_n(c)}\\ &= \log\frac{P(c\mid b)}{P(c\mid a)}. \end{aligned} $$

    $K$ and $P_n(c)$ disappear. That is, $q_b - q_a$ is the amount that represents how much the log association changes when going from $a$ to $b$ across multiple contexts.

    If two word pairs show similar association changes across the vocabulary,

    $$ v_b-v_a \approx v_d-v_c $$

    a parallel translation can appear. This is the most important principle for analogy.

    49.2 Parallel transformation of “king - man + woman ≈ queen”#

    A famous example uses the following relationship:

    $$ v_{king}-v_{man} \approx v_{queen}-v_{woman}. $$

    If both the left and right sides express a similar “royalty direction”, we can simplify the equation to

    $$ \boxed{ v_{king}-v_{man}+v_{woman} \approx v_{queen} } $$

    obtain.

                    + royalty
       man  ---------------->  king
        |                         |
        | + gender relation       | + gender relation
        v                         v
      woman ---------------->   queen
                + royalty

    If we read vector arithmetic in plain language, it looks like this:

    1. Find the translation from “man → king” using “king - man”.
    2. Apply this translation starting from “woman”.
    3. Find the actual word that has the cosine closest to the expected destination.

    The interpretation of the equation “literally deleting the male component from the ‘king’ vector” is too strong. It is not guaranteed that the difference vector contains only one pure attribute. The correct claim is that a parallel transformation holds when the context-profile changes of the two relationships are sufficiently parallel.

    49.3 How does a computer find the correct word? — 3CosAdd#

    Given an analogy question,

    man : king :: woman : ?

    first, we create the target point.

    $$ t=v_{king}-v_{man}+v_{woman} $$

    Then, for all candidate words $x$ in the vocabulary,

    $$ \cos(v_x,t) $$

    we calculate and return the largest candidate.

    $$ \hat{x} = \arg\max_{x\notin\{man,king,woman\}} \cos(v_x,t) $$

    It is important to exclude the input three words from the candidates. This standard search method is often called 3CosAdd.

    import numpy as np

# E[word_id] is the fixed word representation used in this analysis.
E_unit = E / np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True).clip(min=1e-12)

target = E[id_king] - E[id_man] + E[id_woman]
target = target / max(np.linalg.norm(target), 1e-12)

scores = E_unit @ target
scores[[id_man, id_king, id_woman]] = -np.inf
answer_ids = np.argsort(scores)[-10:][::-1]

    When the vocabulary is very large, this search can be accelerated using an approximate nearest-neighbor index. It is better to look at not just the top-1 answer, but also the top-10 candidates and the difference in scores. This is because if “queen” is ranked 1st, it is difficult to say that the relationship is strongly identified if the difference with the 2nd place is almost zero.

    49.4 To discover relationships, not just known analogies#

    It is easy to search for relationships like “king - man” that you already know. To find repeating relationships in a corpus, compare the difference vectors of multiple pairs.

    $$ d_i=v_{b_i}-v_{a_i} $$

    A practical method is as follows:

    1. Create a set of candidate word pairs of interest. Restricting by part of speech or entity type can significantly reduce noise and $O(V^2)$ search.
    2. Align all pair differences in the same direction. For example, always subtract “female - male”.
    3. Calculate the cosine similarity or clustering between the difference vectors.
    4. Manually interpret clusters with repeating offsets along with the word pairs.
    5. Use the average direction of the cluster as a new relationship axis and verify if the same direction appears in the held-out pairs.

    For example,

    $$ v_{woman}-v_{man},\quad v_{queen}-v_{king},\quad v_{actress}-v_{actor} $$

    If two words are similar, their average can be used as an estimate of the gender-related direction. Conversely, if only one pair is correct and the others are different, this is closer to a special difference in the general meaning axis.

    If all pair differences are created arbitrarily, there are $V^2$ candidates. In actual discovery work, candidates are narrowed down by using dictionaries, part-of-speech, knowledge graph relationships, or frequency criteria, or approximate search is used.

    49.5 What is a meaning axis?#

    A meaning axis is a tool for reading a high-dimensional space with a single question.

    cold  <------------------------------>  warm
informal <--------------------------->  formal
concrete <--------------------------->  abstract
negative <--------------------------->  positive

    When the centered vector of a word $w$ is decomposed into the axis direction and the remaining direction, it is

    $$ v_w-m = \underbrace{\big((v_w-m)^\top a\big)a}_{\text{component explained by the axis}} + \underbrace{r_w}_{r_w\perp a} $$

    where $a$ is a vector with length 1, and $m$ is the reference point of the axis. The scalar

    $$ z_w=(v_w-m)^\top a $$

    is not the overall meaning of the word, but rather a coordinate for a specific question. Instead of discarding high-dimensional meaning, we obtain a single, interpretable number that can be compared, sorted, and visualized.

    This has a significant meaning.

    • You can sort words in a corpus in a “formal ↔ informal” order.
    • You can compare how the axis score of the same word changes across different time periods or communities.
    • You can summarize which pole of language a document uses by averaging the scores of the words that appear in the document.
    • You can reuse the relationship direction seen in analogies as a measurement tool.

    49.6 SemAxis method: Creating axes with two poles#

    An axis created from a single word pair is sensitive to random meaning differences. A more stable method is to define multiple seed words to define the two poles.

    For example, let’s define the “warmth” axis as:

    $$ S^+=\{warm,hot,heated\},\qquad S^-=\{cold,cool,chilly\} $$

    First, calculate the average vector for each pole.

    $$ \mu^+ = \frac{1}{|S^+|}\sum_{w\in S^+}v_w, \qquad \mu^- = \frac{1}{|S^-|}\sum_{w\in S^-}v_w $$

    Then, create an axis that points from the negative pole to the positive pole.

    $$ \boxed{ a = \frac{\mu^+-\mu^-} {\|\mu^+-\mu^-\|} } $$

    The SemAxis-style score measures how similar a word is to this axis using cosine.

    $$ score_{axis}(w)=\cos(v_w,a) $$

    Alternatively, you can use the signed projection $z_w = (v_w - m)^T a$, where ‘a’ is the axis, and ‘m’ is the midpoint of the two poles.

    $$ m=\frac{\mu^++\mu^-}{2} $$

    Cosine measures the alignment of the direction, while the signed projection emphasizes how much the axis is moved along the chosen reference point.

    This approach mirrors the core procedure in the original SemAxis paper: “embedding construction → average of the opposite pole set → axis generation from the difference of the average → cosine projection of the word onto the axis”. Using multiple poles instead of a single pair can reduce sensitivity to the specific seed selection.

    # First preprocess E in one consistent way.
E_work = E - E.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)  # Centering is optional.

mu_pos = E_work[positive_ids].mean(axis=0)
mu_neg = E_work[negative_ids].mean(axis=0)

axis = mu_pos - mu_neg
axis = axis / max(np.linalg.norm(axis), 1e-12)

E_unit = E_work / np.linalg.norm(
    E_work, axis=1, keepdims=True
).clip(min=1e-12)
axis_scores = E_unit @ axis
ranked_ids = np.argsort(axis_scores)[::-1]

    Whether or not to center the axes, and whether to normalize the word vectors to unit length before calculating the pole averages, are design choices, not necessarily a single correct answer. The code above is a close approximation of the basic SemAxis form, which first calculates the average. The key is to explicitly specify the preprocessing steps, apply them consistently to the seed and evaluation words, and verify that the ranking is stable across different settings.

    49.7 Differences in methods for finding the “meaning” axis#

    Method How to create an axis Advantages Cautions
    Single offset $v_b - v_a$ Simplest and most intuitive Very sensitive to the properties of a single pair
    Matched-pair average $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}(v_{b_i} - v_{a_i})$ Preserves the directional correspondence between relations All pairs must be consistently aligned in direction
    Pole centroid / SemAxis $\mu^+ - \mu^-$ Stable by averaging multiple seeds The choice of the pole determines the definition of the axis itself
    PCA on offsets The first principal component of the pair-difference matrix Data-drivenly finds recurring changes The sign has no meaning, and the meaning of the resulting axis must be interpreted after
    Linear probe The normal vector of the classification boundary that separates the two sets Finds a direction that aligns well with the labels Relies on supervision and regularization, and does not represent a causal axis

    If the hypothesis comes first, the pole centroid is the easiest to explain. If relation pairs already exist, a matched-pair average or PCA is natural. If you need an axis that predicts labels, a linear probe is useful. Do not use every seed pair for both discovery and validation; leave some pairs held out.

    49.8 How to verify a good axis?#

    Creating an axis does not immediately mean that it has a meaningful axis. At least, check the following:

    1. Held-out validity: Do the expected word pairs, which were not used to create the axis, also appear in the correct order?
    2. Seed stability: Does the ranking remain consistent even if you change a few poles or perform bootstrap resampling?
    3. Neighbor coherence: Do the top words at both ends form a consistent relationship from a human perspective?
    4. Frequency robustness: Are the axis scores similar to simple word frequency or vector norm?
    5. Run stability: Does the same conclusion appear even if you change the random seed?

    When comparing two embeddings that have been trained separately, the coordinate systems may be different. You should perform orthogonal Procrustes alignment using a common anchor vocabulary and then compare the axes or changes. Conversely, analysis that treats words and axes together in the same space is not affected by arbitrary orthogonal rotations.

    49.9 Cues to always include when interpreting#

    • Linear analogy is an empirical phenomenon that appears when there are sufficiently repetitive relationships in the corpus, not a law guaranteed by the SGNS objective.
    • One word vector mixes multiple meanings of a polysemous word. It is difficult to separate the financial/bank meaning of “bank” into a single axis score.
    • Meaning axes do not necessarily need to be orthogonal. The “wealth” and “status” axes can strongly correlate in the corpus.
    • High axis scores do not prove essential properties or causal relationships. Measure the corpus’s expression conventions and biases together.
    • The results depend on tokenization, window size, subsampling, noise distribution, and $Q/K$ selection.

    Therefore, the meaning axis is not a “thermometer” to measure the “true meaning” of words,

    A projection tool that reads the meaning space created by a specific corpus, between two specified poles.

    This is the most accurate understanding.

    A brief exercise: Relationship direction and axis#

    Suppose we obtain the following four difference vectors:

    $$ d_1=[1.0,0.1],\quad d_2=[0.9,-0.1],\quad d_3=[1.1,0.0],\quad d_4=[-0.2,1.0]. $$

    1. Can we say that three of these vectors share a single repeating relationship?
    2. What is the simple average axis for this relationship?
    3. What problem arises if we unconditionally average $d_4$?
    Simple solution $d_1, d_2, d_3$ all point in the positive direction of the first coordinate, so they can be considered as belonging to the same relationship cluster. The average is $$ \bar d=\frac{d_1+d_2+d_3}{3}=[1.0,0.0] $$ which is already a unit vector. $d_4$ represents a different relationship that is almost perpendicular. Mixing these would cause the average direction to skew towards $[0.7, 0.25]$, distorting the original repeating relationship. Therefore, before creating an axis, it is necessary to check the consistency of the directions of the pairs and the clusters.

    While analogy and axis may appear as separate techniques, they actually use the same geometry. Analogy finds the direction of the relationship by adding to a single point and finding the next point, while meaning axis projects all points onto this direction to read the order.

    $$ E(w,c)=-q_w^\top k_c $$

    .

    54. Five Key Equations#

    1. score#

    $$ s(w,c)=q_w^\top k_c $$

    2. SGNS objective#

    $$ \log\sigma(q_w^\top k_c) + \sum_{n=1}^{K} \log\sigma(-q_w^\top k_n) $$

    3. optimal score#

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx \log \frac{P(c\mid w)} {K P_n(c)} $$

    4. When using unigram noise#

    $$ q_w^\top k_c \approx PMI(w,c)-\log K $$

    5. gravity form#

    $$ P_{\text{data}}(w,c) \propto P_{\text{data}}(w)P_n(c) \exp(q_w^\top k_c) $$

    By understanding these five equations together, you will have almost fully grasped the core of Word2Vec.

    55. Interpretation of each equation#

    $$ P_{\text{data}}(w)P_n(c) $$

    These equations explain how frequent two entities are.

    $$ \exp(q_w^\top k_c) $$

    explains pair-specific attraction that goes beyond the baseline.

    Therefore, the general type

    $$ P_{\text{data}}(w,c) \propto P_{\text{data}}(w)P_n(c)\exp(q_w^\top k_c) $$

    can be read as follows:

    The degree to which words $w$ and context $c$ co-occur is determined by the product of “center activity” and “noise,” multiplied by the “context baseline” and “latent semantic attraction” defined by center activity × noise.

    In the unigram noise setting, where $P_n(c) = P(c)$, we obtain the familiar $P(w)P(c)\exp(q_w^\top k_c)$. This is the most intuitive way to view the embedding as a gravity model.

    56. Summary of Implementation Considerations#

    If you think of implementing Word2Vec SGNS directly, you only need the following:

    1. Create a vocabulary.
    2. Create positive (center, context) pairs from the corpus.
    3. Create a frequency-based noise distribution.
    4. Look up the query embedding for each center.
    5. Look up the key embedding for each positive/negative context.
    6. Calculate the dot product.
    7. For positive examples, use log sigmoid(score).
    8. For negative examples, use log sigmoid(-score).
    9. Backpropagate.
    10. Repeat this process.

    The result is:

    • The dot product of pairs that appear together increases,
    • Relations that are more frequent than expected are emphasized compared to the baseline,
    • Words with similar context distributions have similar vectors,
    • A low-dimensional embedding geometry is formed.

    Word2Vec is not simply a “algorithm to convert words into vectors.”

    More accurately:

    It is a model that compares the observed word-context interaction with a noise baseline and factorizes the resulting log-odds structure into a low-dimensional query-key dot product.

    Therefore,

    $$ q_w^\top k_c $$
