    The Renaissance: Composition and Proportion Take Center Stage#

    Western art has never stood still. It has changed with ruling elites, religious institutions, everyday life, and the many other circumstances that make one period different from another. During the long stretch of the Middle Ages, roughly the fifth through the fifteenth centuries, much European art was produced within conventions shaped by Christianity. Artists often gave priority to the symbolic meaning of a person or object over a lifelike depiction. Size and position could communicate a figure’s importance; colors, too, carried particular meanings. These conventions placed substantial constraints on an artist’s freedom to invent a personal style or let the imagination roam.

    By the fifteenth century, Western art was entering a new period of flourishing. Medieval feudal arrangements were giving way, increasingly centralized states were emerging, and social and cultural life was changing. In the following century, the Reformation, conventionally dated to Martin Luther’s intervention in 1517, would also challenge established Catholic traditions of image-making. Across art, science, technology, and philosophy, growing attention to human dignity, individuality, and creativity encouraged a vision of people as versatile beings with remarkable possibilities.

    Artists felt these changes, too. They became increasingly interested in human beauty and in depicting the environments and natural world around them more realistically. Developments in science and technology brought further changes: linear perspective, anatomical studies that supported more accurate depictions of the body, and advances in oil painting all helped make composition and proportion subjects of systematic investigation alongside symbolic meaning.

    Interest in the golden ratio grew in this setting. Luca Pacioli, an Italian mathematician, friar, and writer on economics active in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, wrote extensively about mathematics and geometry. In On Divine Proportion (1509), he explored the ratio’s mathematical properties and its applications to art and architecture. Pacioli also tutored Leonardo da Vinci in mathematics. Leonardo was fascinated enough by the subject to provide illustrations for the book himself.

    Around 300 BCE, Euclid described this relationship under the rather sober name “extreme and mean ratio.” The idea is to divide a line into two pieces so that the ratio of the whole line to the longer piece equals the ratio of the longer piece to the shorter one. The resulting ratio is irrational and approximately equal to 1.618:1. Try a line of length 1, divided into pieces of approximately 0.618 and 0.382. Divide the whole, 1, by the longer piece, 0.618, and you get about 1.618. Divide the longer piece, 0.618, by the shorter piece, 0.382, and you again get about 1.618. The ratio was not always a household name. Pacioli’s book helped introduce it to artists and architects under a name with considerably more mystique: the divine proportion.

    The familiar “golden” label is much more recent. The German mathematician Martin Ohm is commonly credited with an early use of “golden section”—goldener Schnitt—in his 1835 book Die reine Elementar-Mathematik. And yes, he was the younger brother of Georg Ohm, whose law you may remember from school lessons about electric current. Perhaps the name itself helped: the notion that this was the most beautiful of all proportions spread, along with claims that hidden golden ratios could be found in the Parthenon, the pyramids at Giza, and Leonardo’s paintings. Until fairly recently, such claims even appeared in Korean school textbooks. For many of these buildings and artworks, however, evidence that their makers actually used the ratio is weak. Even in Leonardo’s work—and he certainly knew about the ratio—convincing evidence of its deliberate application remains elusive. What we can say with confidence is that Leonardo, as both artist and scientist, sought to understand harmony and beauty mathematically. His investigations of the human body, nature, painting, and music reveal a sustained interest in the proportions and relationships they might share.

    Rediscovering Proportions in Paintings with Science#

    So, were there proportions that painters throughout Western art history actually considered beautiful? Did they favor the golden ratio, consciously or otherwise? Or did they prefer something else? If a preference existed, did it survive across periods and places, or did it change?

    During my PhD in statistical physics, my colleagues and I set out to answer these questions with data. We quantified compositional proportions in roughly 15,000 landscape paintings, looking for the arrangements and ratios painters had used over more than five centuries. At the time, Professor Seung-Kee Han’s group at Chungbuk National University was developing an information-theoretic approach to composition and complexity in paintings. Our own laboratory had been conducting large-scale quantitative analyses of artworks. The collaboration was a natural fit.

    To characterize composition, we used an improved version of an image-partitioning algorithm previously proposed by a team at the University of Girona in Spain. Its basic idea was to use information theory to find a horizontal or vertical line that separated a painting into two regions with the most distinct color distributions. There was one catch: the earlier method was slow. Processing tens of thousands of paintings would take a very long time. Fortunately, Professor Han’s group had just published a substantial improvement, making large-scale analysis practical. We focused on landscapes. They often contain a prominent horizontal boundary between sky and land or sea, along with vertical structures such as buildings and trees, making them a good fit for this method. Restricting the subject matter also let us compare how different painters approached the same broad task: composing a landscape. First, though, we needed images and their accompanying records. I wrote a web-scraping program to collect publicly available material from two online galleries. We assembled about 15,000 landscape paintings, together with information about their artists, dates, and countries.

    After a lengthy stretch of data collection and cleaning, we were ready to start dividing paintings. But wait: how can an algorithm decide where a painting should be split? For that, we need a little information theory. In this framework, information is connected to uncertainty. Information is uncertainty? That sounds backward. Let’s visit two imaginary villages. It rains every day in Village A. The evening weather report announces, “Tomorrow will be cloudy, with rain.” For the villagers, this is hardly news. Rain was already a certainty. Now consider Village B, where it rains on half the days of the year. When its forecast says, “It will rain tomorrow,” the message is useful. Tomorrow’s weather was uncertain; the report resolves that fifty-fifty uncertainty. The greater the uncertainty about the weather, the more a useful weather report can tell us. That is the connection we need.

    We can think about color information in much the same way. A digital photograph of a painting consists of pixels with different color values. In this sense, an image containing many different colors has high uncertainty about color: pick a pixel at random, and its color is difficult to predict. For a simpler example, imagine an image whose upper half is entirely red and whose lower half is entirely blue. Choose a pixel at random. What color will it be? Because the two colors occupy equal areas, guessing either red or blue gives you exactly a 50 percent chance of being right. There is uncertainty about the pixel’s color.

    Now suppose we find a line that neatly divides the image into its two regions. Draw it across the middle. The upper region is entirely red; the lower region is entirely blue. Once we know which region the pixel came from, we know its color. Dividing the image has removed the uncertainty! Before the split, the pixel’s color was uncertain. After the split, its region tells us the answer. The partition therefore provides information about color. The more effectively a dividing line separates regions with different colors, the more uncertainty it can remove and the more color information it can reveal. We call this compositional information: information supplied by the image’s spatial organization. The best line can fall anywhere, depending on how the painting uses color. The algorithm tests many possible divisions and selects the line that yields the most information. For Claude Lorrain’s landscape in Figure 2, the most informative horizontal split falls at the 227th pixel from the top of an image 373 pixels high. This gives a relative partition position of 227:373, or approximately 0.608. That number is the painting’s compositional ratio. It is the same kind of part-to-whole measurement as the approximately 0.618 fraction occupied by the longer piece in a golden-ratio division. Repeating the procedure divides the painting into smaller and smaller regions, producing something like the puzzle pieces in the lower panels of Figure 2.

    Figure 2. Finding the dominant compositional ratio with information-theoretic partitioning. The painting is Claude Lorrain’s Seaport with the Embarkation of the Queen of Sheba (1648).

    How, then, did the ratios painters used change across artists and periods? Because most landscapes were first divided horizontally, we examined the distribution of the dominant horizontal boundary: a line across which the color pattern changes sharply, often separating sky from land or another strongly contrasting region. As Figure 3 shows, certain ratios were indeed more common at particular times, and the preferred ratios changed remarkably gradually. The golden ratio, however, does not seem to have received special treatment from landscape painters.

    Figure 3. Changes in horizontal partition ratios across Western art history, grouped by year, artistic movement, and artist.

    In seventeenth-century Baroque landscapes, the dominant horizontal boundary was most often low in the picture, below the halfway point. During the Rococo and Romantic periods, a position near the middle became more popular. The boundary then moved progressively upward: from the nineteenth century onward, the most frequent position was roughly one-third of the way down from the top. Intriguingly, this preference for a one-third division appeared across several modern artistic movements. Modern art is famous for its proliferation of isms, each pursuing distinct ways of using color and representing the world. Yet these movements often shared similar compositional proportions. Diversity in style did not necessarily mean diversity in where painters placed a dominant horizontal boundary.

    The algorithm also tells us about composition beyond a single ratio. Apply it twice, and landscapes can be classified into four types according to the directions of the two splits. If the most informative first split is horizontal and the next is vertical, we call the arrangement horizontal–vertical, or H–V. Figure 4 shows representative paintings from the four categories.

    Figure 4. Representative landscape compositions characterized by the directions of their partitions.

    Figure 5. Gradual changes in the use of four landscape composition types across Western art history. From the seventeenth century to the mid-nineteenth century, horizontal–vertical (H–V) compositions were the most common. Horizontal–horizontal (H–H) compositions became steadily more frequent and took the lead after the mid-nineteenth century.

    These four compositional types did not keep the same popularity throughout art history. As Figure 5 shows, landscapes from the seventeenth century to the mid-nineteenth century most often combined a horizontal structure with a vertical one: the H–V arrangement. Over time, that balance shifted. After the mid-nineteenth century, H–H compositions, with two horizontal divisions suggestive of foreground, middle ground, and background, became the most common. Similar patterns of change appeared across several European countries.

    Does a Universal Golden Ratio Exist?#

    Our study did not find a golden-ratio signal that transcended time and place. But analyzing some 15,000 landscapes did reveal something else: painters in different periods favored particular proportions, gradually adopting new ratios and compositional arrangements. As we approached the present, the range of compositions and proportions used by individual artists also tended to increase.

    This is one reason such research matters. Physics and mathematics may seem remote from the humanities, yet they can help us approach questions about art that people have been asking for a very long time. Just as scientific and technological developments opened new possibilities for Renaissance artists, today’s tools can reveal patterns and relationships hidden within creative expression.

    Interest in composition and proportion certainly has not faded. What arrangements appear in the photographs we share on social media? Take another look at the picture you just posted on Instagram. Even in a photo composed without much conscious thought, there may be a pattern waiting to be noticed.
