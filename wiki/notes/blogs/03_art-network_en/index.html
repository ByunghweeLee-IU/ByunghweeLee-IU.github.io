Art Through Data (3): The Secret of Success—Networks Behind Artists’ Reputations

Byunghwee Lee

    The story of Jean-Michel Basquiat and Al Diaz, two friends who made their names with graffiti on the streets of Manhattan, offers a striking introduction to the importance of networks in the art market. As teenagers, they formed a partnership called SAMO and took their spray paint and crayons around SoHo, leaving work on its streets. Eventually, for a variety of reasons, the two went their separate ways. They had lived and worked in the same neighborhood, yet their futures would turn out very differently. Diaz did not seek fame. Basquiat, meanwhile, kept meeting artists and building new connections. He befriended Keith Haring in New York’s art world, and the two shared their artistic visions. In the 1980s, he also met Andy Warhol, already a world-famous pioneer of Pop Art, and they went on to produce a number of works together. Warhol offered Basquiat advice about networking and marketing in the art world, while his commercial success and reputation provided inspiration. Basquiat died tragically young, at just 27, but the prices of his paintings have continued to climb. At a Sotheby’s auction in 2017, one sold for approximately ₩125 billion, setting an auction record for the artist. The story of Basquiat and Diaz shows how differently artists and their work can come to be valued—even when they begin in similar surroundings, working in similar styles—as the networks around them diverge.

    When we assess a work of art, then, we are rarely looking at the object alone. We also attend to the context and stories surrounding the work and its maker. Whether a painting reaches a prestigious museum, and how much someone pays for it, can depend heavily on a vast network that is largely invisible to us. Curators, art historians, museum directors, dealers, auction houses, and collectors all have a place in it. A study published in Science in 2018 by a team including Albert-László Barabási and Christoph Riedl put some of these relationships under a scientific microscope. Their question was how networks help shape success and reputation in the art world.

    The Key to Success Lies in the Network#

    Using Magnus, an app containing exhibition histories and auction records, the researchers collected exhibition data for approximately 496,000 artists. The first thing that stood out was just how uneven the distributions were—both in exhibition opportunities and in the prices that works fetched. More than half of the artists, about 52%, had only a single recorded exhibition. More than half of the works sold for $4,000 or less. At an illustrative exchange rate of ₩1,300 to the dollar, that is no more than ₩5.2 million per work. At the other end of the distribution, however, some works sold for as much as $110 million—more than ₩140 billion at that same exchange rate. The art market has quite a range.

    Why should the prices of different artists’ works vary so much? Why do some artists succeed while so many others disappear from the exhibition record after one or two shows? One plausible factor is where the work is exhibited. Prestigious institutions such as the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) want to invite famous, sought-after artists, and influential artists want to exhibit at prestigious institutions. If we could identify the institutions that occupy these influential positions, we might be better able to understand artists’ prospects. But there is a catch. Museums do not line up on an Olympic podium with gold, silver, and bronze medals around their necks. Judging their reputations objectively is much harder.

    The researchers tackled this problem with a network. Using artists’ exhibition histories, they built a coexhibition network of museums and galleries. Each node, or point, represents an institution. A directed link between two institutions captures how extensively they share artists in sequence: artists who exhibit at one go on to exhibit at the other. In this way, the team assembled a vast network containing 16,002 galleries, 7,568 museums, and approximately 19 million links.

    Figure 1. A museum and gallery coexhibition network constructed from artists’ exhibition histories. Source: Figure 1 of reference [1], DOI:10.1126/science.aau7224.

    The network in Figure 1 brings some normally hidden features of the art world into view. Several clusters of similarly colored nodes immediately catch the eye. At the network’s core is a dense grouping made up largely of institutions in Europe and North America. These prominent institutions are tightly connected through a shared pool of artists. Artist flows between many of the hub institutions in this group were exceptionally strong. The connection between MoMA and the Guggenheim in New York, for example, was 33 times stronger than would be expected if artists moved randomly. One interpretation is that artists who enter this core of major European and North American institutions are especially likely to keep exhibiting within it. The network also contains dense regional clusters in Europe, Asia, South America, and Australia. These regional groups were relatively isolated from the core and shared distinct pools of artists.

    But did the institutions at the center of this network actually enjoy strong reputations? The Magnus data included expert ratings, from A to D, for roughly 9,000 institutions, so the researchers could compare these ratings with positions in the coexhibition network. One way to identify influential nodes is eigenvector centrality. The idea is that a node’s importance depends on how important its neighbors are. Being connected to several well-connected hubs contributes more to your centrality than being connected to neighbors with relatively few connections. Institutions with high eigenvector centrality tended to receive high expert ratings, and the upper end of the prices associated with them was also higher.

    Artists’ Careers on the Coexhibition Network#

    Could this network help predict artists’ careers? To investigate, the researchers used exhibition histories to define a measure of reputation. An artist’s initial reputation was the average network-based prestige of the institutions that hosted their first five exhibitions. Artists whose average placed them within the top 20% of the institutional ranking formed the “high initial reputation” group; those whose average fell within the bottom 40% formed the “low initial reputation” group. These early differences were followed by sharply different outcomes. Ten years after their fifth exhibition, 39% of the high-initial-reputation group were still active, compared with only 14% of the low-initial-reputation group.

    How long did those early reputations last? Looking at 31,794 artists born between 1950 and 1990, each with at least ten exhibitions, the team compared their early and late careers. Among artists who began with high reputations, 58.6% were still exhibiting at high-reputation institutions later in their careers. Only 0.2% had moved into the low-reputation group, averaging within the bottom 40% of the institutional ranking. Of the artists who started with low reputations, just 10.2% reached the high-reputation group later on. Works by artists in the high-initial-reputation group appeared in auction sales 4.7 times as often, and their maximum auction prices were 5.2 times as high. They also held 49% of their exhibitions abroad, compared with 37% for the low-initial-reputation group—a difference of 12 percentage points. Early reputation, it seems, has a surprisingly long memory. The first few exhibitions already contained information that helped predict an artist’s longer-term trajectory. In network terms, these careers exhibited strong path dependence: artists who reached the core tended to remain there, while those who began in peripheral groups tended to keep circling the periphery.

    The researchers proposed two models to explore how this pattern could arise. In the first, artists moved randomly around the coexhibition network—a random walk. The second was a memory model (Figure 2), which assigned weights according to the reputations of institutions in an artist’s exhibition history. Here, the past could influence the present. In the random-walk model, artists eventually converged on institutions of median prestige, unlike the pattern in the data. The memory model, by contrast, reproduced the persistence of initial reputation much more closely. Accounting for past exhibitions helped explain and predict future exhibition trajectories (Figure 3).

    Figure 2. Different paths, different career prospects. Each point represents an institution where an artist exhibited, and the arrows indicate the order of exhibitions. In a model with memory, two artists can exhibit at the same institution yet face different future prospects because they arrived there by different routes.

    Artists Who Found a Way Through#

    Does this mean that an artist who starts in low-reputation institutions can never exhibit at prestigious ones? Fortunately, the odds may be small, but they are not zero. Some artists did climb from the low-initial-reputation group into the high-late-reputation group. How did they get through the art world’s glass ceiling? These artists had several things in common. They generally reached the high-reputation group within a relatively short period—less than ten years. Compared with others who had started in the same low-reputation group, they exhibited at a wider variety of institutions and across a broader range of institutional reputations. The institutions where they showed their work were also closer, in network distance, to highly prestigious museums such as MoMA. In other words, getting out into a broader range of venues early in a career was associated with a better chance of later reaching high-reputation institutions.

    Closing Thoughts#

    In this chapter, we have taken a look at the network behind the art world. A map made of simple points and lines turns out to carry valuable information about how institutions connect and how artists’ careers unfold. It reveals an art world organized into clusters, some of which join together to form a powerful core. The trouble is that these core and peripheral groups can behave a little like gravitational traps. Artists who reached the core tended to continue exhibiting at prominent institutions; those who began on the periphery tended to remain there. Artists in the core stayed active longer, sold their work for higher prices, and found opportunities to exhibit in new countries.

    These results show how strongly early conditions are associated with later outcomes in the art market, and how unequal those outcomes can become. It is an uncomfortable pattern. Yet understanding it may also help us imagine a more diverse and sustainable art world. One possibility is to support opportunities for young artists to exhibit at a wider variety of institutions. On our network map, this means helping them visit a broader range of nodes and, potentially, opening up more future paths. Artists with more varied exhibition histories did have a greater chance of later building high reputations. Another possibility is to create more connections between institutions—to add new links to the map. Because these links depend on how many artists exhibit at both institutions, a touring exhibition that brings well-known artists from one museum to another could also help raise the receiving institution’s standing. Supporting more exchanges across a wider variety of institutions might therefore help soften the network’s unequal distribution of reputation. How to use this map is now up to us.
