Art Through Data (4): The Secrets of Impressionism—Air Pollution in Turner and Monet's Paintings

    The Natural Environment on Canvas#

    The Industrial Revolution transformed Europe’s atmosphere during the nineteenth century. Rapid industrialization left major cities such as London and Paris struggling with severe air pollution, changing the world their artists saw. In a 2023 paper in PNAS, Anna Lea Albright and Peter Huybers investigated the relationship between these changes and the painting styles of Joseph Mallord William Turner and Claude Monet. Their study reveals an intriguing connection between works of art and the environments in which they were made [1].

    Modern painters often departed from realistic representation to give their individual voices and ways of seeing more room. Yet research suggests that some paintings, however far they seem from realism, faithfully record natural phenomena. Even familiar works by Vincent van Gogh and Edvard Munch may preserve detailed observations of the atmosphere around them.

    By analyzing topography and the phases of the Moon, researchers dated the scene in Van Gogh’s Moonrise to a remarkably precise moment: July 13, 1889, at 9:08 p.m. His bold, original brushwork coexisted with a close account of the natural world he witnessed [2]. Another study proposed that the sky in Munch’s The Scream, with its dreamlike setting and otherworldly figure, resembles the colors of nacreous clouds—also called mother-of-pearl clouds—and may capture that atmospheric phenomenon [3]. These are not isolated examples. Researchers used the geometry of the Sun and light in nine paintings from Monet’s London series to determine when their scenes were observed, obtaining dates and times consistent with his letters [4]. Other research found that the ratio of red to green in sunset paintings by various artists correlated with the amount of stratospheric aerosol associated with volcanic activity [5].

    Figure 1. Nacreous clouds (left) and the sky in Munch’s The Scream (right). Source: S. M. Fikke, J. E. Kristjánsson, Ø. Nordli, Screaming clouds. Weather 72, 115–121 (2017).

    Natural phenomena have shaped many artists’ work, and environmental changes across times and places may also have encouraged new creative expressions. Albright and Huybers show that this relationship between surroundings and style can be explored in the familiar paintings of Turner and Monet, too.

    The Industrial Revolution and the Changing Styles of Turner and Monet#

    Turner and Monet are celebrated figures in British and French painting, loved far beyond their own countries and times. Turner was born in 1775 and worked during the early nineteenth century; Monet was born in 1840 and worked during its later decades. Both frequently painted landscapes and cityscapes, returning to recurring motifs as their styles gradually changed. Their paintings moved from sharp to hazy contours, strong contrasts to softer pastel tones, and object-centered depictions toward more impressionistic representations. Albright and Huybers explored an intriguing hypothesis: the changes seen in Turner, Monet, and other painters reflected, at least in part, physical changes in the optical properties of the atmosphere.

    The Industrial Revolution and Air Pollution#

    Following the Industrial Revolution, Britain’s aerosol emissions rose to unprecedented levels during the nineteenth century, substantially changing its atmosphere. Aerosols are tiny particles suspended in the air, including those associated with industrial emissions. Their concentration affects the optical properties of the air we look through. Aerosol particles absorb and scatter light, tending to blur the boundaries between objects and reduce contrast.

    Figure 2. London smog. London. Embankment.; Frederick H. Evans (British, 1853–1943); 1908.

    Most factories in the early Industrial Revolution ran on coal, which typically contained about 1–5% sulfur. Burning it released sulfur dioxide, or SO₂. Between 1800 and 1850, Britain accounted for nearly half of the world’s SO₂ emissions. Although SO₂ is a gas, its emissions provide a useful proxy for historical aerosol pollution, which involves a mixture of substances. The researchers therefore used estimated SO₂ emissions to infer broad changes in aerosol concentrations.

    This pollution tends to soften outlines, reduce contrast, and make objects look whiter and hazier. If painters recorded these atmospheric conditions, their works should likewise show lower contrast and greater brightness. The researchers used a wavelet technique to quantify contrast in the paintings and examine its relationship with aerosol pollution, alongside changes in brightness. As SO₂ emissions increased, contrast in the paintings decreased significantly. Brightness also correlated with emissions, although the paper reports that this relationship was noisier and less statistically clear than the contrast result.

    Figure 3. (Top) Contrast in impressionistic paintings over time. (Bottom) SO₂ emissions and painting contrast. Higher emissions, used as a proxy for aerosol pollution, are associated with lower contrast in the paintings.

    The team built a statistical model to explain contrast in Turner’s and Monet’s paintings. With painting year, SO₂ emissions, subject type—such as a clear sky, cloudy sky, or dawn—and the interaction between year and emissions as predictors, the model explained about 61% of the variation in contrast. A model using time alone explained about 43%. This offers indirect evidence that regional differences in air pollution, rather than the passage of time alone, were reflected in the paintings.

    For example, London’s SO₂ emissions around 1796, the beginning of the Turner paintings studied, were similar to Paris’s emissions around 1864, the beginning of the Monet paintings studied. Monet’s early Paris paintings consequently show higher contrast than most of Turner’s, even though they were painted later. This pattern is consistent with London’s air pollution having worsened much earlier than Paris’s.

    The Environmental Context Within Art#

    This study of air pollution in Turner and Monet’s paintings shows what art and science can discover together. Albright and Huybers suggest that artworks hold more than aesthetic value: they can also reflect the environmental conditions of their time. Art offers beauty, but it also carries traces of how people lived and the worlds they inhabited. Analyzing atmospheric optics alongside painting style suggests that these works can be both moving landscapes and historical records of changes in nineteenth-century air. Such analysis may help us reconstruct environments we never experienced and deepen our awareness of environmental problems today.

    Some readers might wonder whether explaining stylistic change through faithful observation of a changing environment diminishes the achievement of impressionistic painting. The authors emphasize that their findings do not devalue the creativity of Turner, Monet, or the other artists discussed. Instead, they suggest that the close relationship between art and environment gave painters new ways of seeing. Environmental change may have supplied a “creative impulse,” encouraging artists to move away from defining objects through lines and edges and toward distinguishing them through color and light.

    The Industrial Revolution is hardly the only example of a changing world opening up creative possibilities. Trains, paint tubes, and cameras all emerged from scientific and technological developments that interacted, directly or indirectly, with artistic practice. Those interactions helped bring new forms of expression and imagination into being. I look forward to seeing what new kinds of artistic creativity the changes ahead of us will inspire.
